# How to judge a fractional or interim product leader

*For founders, CEOs and investors hiring one.* The rest of this repository is written from the candidate's side: how to judge whether a role deserves commitment. This page turns the same logic around. It is one practitioner's view, written in November 2026, not a validated method.

A permanent hire is judged on whether they will grow into the job. An interim or fractional product leader has weeks, not years, so the questions change: how fast can they find the real constraint, what will they decide, and what will still work after they leave?

## 1. Agree the mandate before you judge the person

Most interim mandates fail on the mandate, not the person. Before interviewing, write down:

- **The outcome:** what must be true after 90 days. Not "fix product", but "customers see changes they asked for within a month" or "the roadmap has three bets the board can follow".
- **Decision rights:** what the interim decides alone (priorities, the roadmap, team structure, hiring), and what goes back to the CEO or the board.
- **Sponsorship:** who backs them when the decisions are unpopular, and what happens when the CEO and the investor disagree.
- **The handover:** stabilise and hand over, turn around, or prepare for the permanent leader?

If you cannot write these down, a stronger candidate will not save the engagement. An interim who is accountable for results but bypassed on decisions is set up to fail.

## 2. Ask for the playbook, then test it against your situation

Good interim leaders have a way of going in that they have used before. Ask: *"Walk me through your first ten days, forty-five days and ninety days here."* Listen for:

- **Stabilising before changing:** what stays the same while they learn.
- **Diagnosing in the open:** time with customers, sales, support and engineering, not only with the product team; the commercial picture and the product picture together.
- **A deliverable early:** a short diagnosis with the main constraints and the first decisions, within about two weeks.
- **Visible decisions on evidence:** something stopped, re-sequenced or settled, and why. Speed is the time between learning and acting, not theatre.
- **Making it last:** a rhythm the team runs without them, and a handover to whoever comes next.

A generic answer that would fit any company is a warning sign. So is one that starts by installing a framework.

## 3. Ask for decisions, not responsibilities

CVs list scope. Interviews should surface decisions. Ask for one decision they made to **stop** something, one they made **against** a senior stakeholder's wish, and one they got **wrong**. For each: what they decided, what they personally did, and what happened. If there is no honest number, ask what the evidence was.

A candidate who can only describe teams they led and transformations they "drove" has told you their scope, not their judgment.

## 4. Check how they handle the team they inherit

Interim leaders often arrive after someone has been removed. Ask how they would earn the trust of a team loyal to their predecessor, which people they would protect, and what they would not change in the first month. The answer shows whether they lead through context or through control.

## 5. Look for checks, not confidence

Ask how they know when they are wrong. The strongest answers describe a mechanism: customer evidence reviewed weekly, a person other than the builder checking the work, a rule written down when something goes wrong so it cannot happen twice. Confidence without a mechanism is a risk in a short engagement, because there is no time to discover the mistake slowly.

## 6. Be clear about the terms from the start

Days per week, duration, notice on both sides, who pays the intermediary and how, and whether the role could become permanent. Fractional leaders working two or three days a week usually charge a higher day rate than full-time interims, because they carry the gaps between engagements. Settle this early; it shapes who applies.
