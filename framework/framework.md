# Job Scoring Framework — v6.2

*A decision tool, not an essay. It exists to predict organizational fit and to identify where capability can create unusual value — not to explain organizations beautifully.*

**Architecture:** A strategic layer says what the search is for. Principles state commitments. Gates stop bad fits. The positive lens finds asymmetry. Nine factors predict fit. Diagnostics and heuristics operationalize the factors. Evidence and usage rules keep the scoring honest. A calibration appendix holds dated examples so the framework itself stays stable.

---

## 0. STRATEGIC LAYER — CAREER PORTFOLIO (October 2026)

**Guiding statement.** Find organizations with problems I'm unusually well equipped to solve, that recognize and will pay for my contribution, and where solving those problems doesn't require sacrificing the rest of my life.

The scoring machine below answers *is this role worth it?* This layer answers *what is the search for?* It governs **which opportunities deserve pursuit and how work fits into life**. It is not a tenth organizational-fit factor, and it does not change the gates, weights, asymmetry overlay or candidate-fit rating.

**1. Value creation ≠ value capture.** Being unusually capable of solving a problem (Asymmetric Fit) is different from being hired and paid to solve it (Candidate Fit, channel, salary and authorization gates). For each serious opportunity, identify:
- **Problem owner:** who experiences the problem and has reason to want it fixed?
- **Budget owner:** who can approve the headcount, compensation and investment? This might not be the problem owner. *Probe:* "Who feels this problem most, and who decides the budget to fix it?"
- **Selection decision-maker:** who must believe my specific experience is the best answer, and what evidence would convince them?
- **Recognition:** is the problem acknowledged as important now, and does the buyer recognize my *literal proof* rather than require an analogy they may not accept?

Use concrete outcomes and personal interventions, not adjectives, in applications and interviews. **But communication has a ceiling.** The calibration appendix shows that every rejection with a known reason turned on a literal requirement or a level/archetype mismatch, never on the quality of the argument. Better framing cannot create recognition where a literal requirement is missing; an advocate, or choosing roles where recognition is already likely, can.

Rejections about direct domain expertise are **market evidence about selection and recognition**, not proof that my value-creation assessment or the organization's fit was wrong; they update candidate-fit calibration, per the outcome-update rule. Do not count recognition, budget and salary again in the nine-factor total.

**2. Timing and optionality.** Judge a path on two horizons: **what is viable now** and **what compounds into a desirable direction over several years**. A strong long-term fit can be inaccessible, underfunded or badly timed today. Maintain credible ways to earn and learn — permanent Product leadership, fractional or bounded coaching work, teaching and other self-generated work — without turning any one channel into a permanent identity. This is what makes the **Gig** verdict and the **real alternative** usable rather than theoretical. Test interests and capabilities against labor-market responses through small, reversible experiments, not narrative alone; a bounded gig or a teaching engagement can be an experiment, not only income. Evaluate this **across the portfolio**, rather than penalizing an individual employer's organizational score because other options are currently scarce.

**3. Life purpose ≠ employer's business model.** Purpose lives across family, community, friendships and interests outside work, including joy in ridiculous things. Mentoring, teaching and community organising may express some of it better outside employment; this is one reason Work interest carries 5%, not more. Work should offer enough meaning and contribution to remain sustainable, **while supporting rather than monopolizing that wider life**. Watch for employers that require more attention, health or family time than the work can sustainably give back: score that in **Sustainability** (look for its cause in the fear diagnostic), and do not let a strong mission or a good salary compensate for it. Geography and ethical limits remain hard gates; ongoing demands belong in Sustainability and the final *Home/Gig* judgment.

**Strategic decision test:** *Can I create unusual value? Will this buyer recognize and reward it now? Does the opportunity preserve enough of the life I want to live? What do I gain or give up over the next few years?*

## 1. LEADERSHIP AND CAREER PRINCIPLES

**Effort should compound.**
Hard work should produce durable value: learning, capability, trust, better decisions, better products, stronger teams. Failure can still compound when it generates genuine learning. What is intolerable is not intensity but effort the system repeatedly absorbs without producing either value or learning.

**Leaders cultivate cognitive ecology.**
Leadership shapes the social, emotional, informational, and structural conditions under which people notice, reason, disagree, learn, and decide together. Translation tax, political safety, learning loops, sustainability, and fear are different manifestations of this underlying environment.

**Protect conditions, not solutions.**
Strong leaders do not solve every problem for experts. They establish priorities, protect attention, clarify constraints, remove unnecessary interference, and ensure authority matches accountability. They intervene decisively when the conditions themselves are failing. *Soil before water:* before adding an intervention, identify the limiting condition — sometimes water is genuinely missing; sometimes the soil cannot absorb it.

**Reality before reassurance.**
A healthy organization would rather learn an uncomfortable truth than preserve a comforting story. It distinguishes uncertainty from failure, signal from noise, and evidence from theatre. It trusts expertise even when expertise contradicts leadership's initial intuition.

**Worthy of my effort.**
The career question is not merely whether a role is attractive, but whether the organization merits the appropriate level of commitment: several years for a potential home, or a clearly bounded period for a deliberate gig.

## 2. GATES (binary — any failure stops the process)

No factor score compensates for a failed gate.

1. **Salary floor** — your minimum monthly base, set before you look at any role. Confirm it is *base cash*, not base-plus-illiquid-equity. Clearing only at the top of a band, or only by counting startup options, is a conditional pass at best.
2. **Geography** — compatible with Helsinki family life. "Regular / at least 20% / sometimes more" travel is elastic and a real sustainability question; bounded predictable travel is fine.
3. **Role is real, authorized, live, and genuinely open to an external hire.** A public posting is not required — legitimate executive searches and network-originated roles often never appear publicly. What fails: speculative interest, pipeline-shaping conversations, unfunded roles, and searches where an internal or preselected candidate effectively owns the seat.
   *This gate is hard to judge from outside.* Two roles in my calibration set looked open and were not: one already had another candidate in late stages; in the other, the person who made the introduction took the seat. **Ask early:** *"How far along is the process, and is anyone already in late stages or internal?"* A recruiter who answers vaguely is itself information. **Then:** *"Is this role in the approved headcount plan for the next 12 months, and has the company changed its hiring plan this year?"* This catches the unfunded or soon-to-be-cut seat: one company in the calibration set was hiring and announced layoffs within weeks, a sign that hiring managers did not control their own headcount.
4. **No obvious dealbreakers** — no ethical, reputational, or structural red flag you would walk away from regardless of score.

**FAIL vs UNRESOLVED.**
- **FAIL → stop.** No scoring, no drafting.
- **UNRESOLVED** (typically salary unstated, or geography ambiguous) → scoring may continue **provisionally**, but the verdict is **conditional**, and the unresolved gate is surfaced first, above the score, with the specific question that resolves it. Draft application material only if the human explicitly chooses to invest before resolution.
- An UNRESOLVED gate that later resolves as FAIL voids the score. It does not become a reason to reconsider the floor.

## 3. POSITIVE SELECTION LENS: ASYMMETRIC FIT

*The framework's counterweight to threat detection. The nine factors screen out environments that degrade capability; this lens seeks environments where capability creates unusual value.*

**Where can I create disproportionate value relative to a broadly qualified senior product leader?**

Asymmetry exists only when all three conditions hold:

1. **Rarity** — is my combination of experience genuinely uncommon among plausible candidates?
2. **Relevance** — does that combination map directly to a current, important organizational problem?
3. **Deployability** — do the mandate, sponsor, and operating environment allow the organization to actually use that advantage?

*The third is critical. Rarity without relevance is novelty. Relevance without rarity is ordinary qualification. Rarity and relevance without deployability produce frustration, not leverage — an organization can hire capabilities it nominally needs and lack the capacity to absorb or deploy them.*

**Builder Shape Fit (part of relevance and deployability).** *Individuals should be spiky; teams should be well-rounded.* A role is especially attractive when its hardest recurring problems exercise the candidate's strongest competencies, while complementary people, systems or agents cover the weaker ones. A rare background is not genuinely asymmetric if the job spends most of its time on competencies that are adequate or weak, while the spikes go unused.
> **Probe:** *"What will the person in this role spend disproportionate time being unusually good at?"*
> **Inputs.** Spikes come from self-assessment; **gaps come from selection evidence**, not self-assessment, because self-rating reliably under-reports gaps. Keep both lists in the search profile.
> A role whose critical path runs through the weak zone, with no team or agents to cover it (solo PM, only builder, one team with no reports), is low shape fit however rare the background. *Illustration:* one role puts data, experience, direction and executive alignment on the critical path; another puts service operations, procurement and contracts there — close to the inverse.

**Scoring (overlay, not part of the numerical total):**
- **0 — Generic:** credible but broadly substitutable.
- **1 — Differentiated:** several relevant strengths combine unusually well.
- **2 — Asymmetric:** rare stack, urgent problem, and clear capacity to deploy it.

**Upgrade rule:** an asymmetry score of 2 may upgrade Viable → Strong, **and only when Mandate ≥ 7**, so deployability is structurally enforced rather than self-assessed. Asymmetry can never rescue a failed gate or a load-bearing warning. This prevents the attractive uniqueness of a role from rationalizing structural danger.

*Scope clarification.* Asymmetry measures **value creation** — whether you would be unusually useful *once hired*. It does not predict whether you will be hired. "Relevance" here means relevance as you can defend it, which is often structural analogy. How the hiring team will read that analogy is a separate question, scored in section 3b.

## 3b. SECOND AXIS: CANDIDATE FIT

*Organizational fit (the nine factors) answers: **would I thrive there?** Candidate fit answers a different question: **will they pick me?** The two are scored separately and must never be combined into one number.*

**Why separate.** A role you would thrive in but are unlikely to get should keep its high organizational score, because that score carries true information about where you do your best work. Lowering it for poor odds would destroy that information. Conversely, a role you would easily get but would not thrive in must not have its low organizational score rescued by high odds. Folding the axes together corrupts both.

**Why it exists.** Asymmetry was quietly doing double duty — its "relevance" leg was self-assessed on structural analogy, and it was being read as a hiring prediction. The evidence against that reading is consistent: in every case where the rejection reason is known, it was a **literal stated requirement already identified as a gap**, not a weakness in the analogy.

**Buyer map — selection diagnostic, not a fourth score.** For a serious opportunity, identify who owns the problem, who owns the budget or hiring authorization, and who makes the selection decision. They may be different people. Check that the problem is recognized *now* and that the decision-maker sees literal evidence of this candidate as its solution. Missing budget belongs to the hard gate; missing recognition belongs to Candidate Fit, positioning and channel strategy. It does **not** lower Organizational Fit or Asymmetry. See the Strategic Layer.

**Scored on literal evidence, not analogy:**
- **Stated requirements met from the fact base** — required versus nice-to-have, counted rather than argued
- **Direct domain or product-pattern match** — has this exact thing been done, not something structurally similar
- **Level match** — is the candidate where the role is targeted

**Step one — identify the selection-critical requirement(s).** Not all required bullets are equal. Before rating, name the **one or two qualifications most likely to separate finalists** — usually the requirement that is most specific to this product, stated most emphatically, or repeated across the JD. Generic requirements (years of experience, communication, leadership) rarely decide it.
- If a selection-critical requirement is **not met literally**, candidate fit **cannot be High.**
- If a selection-critical requirement is **central to the actual product** and is met **only by analogy**, candidate fit defaults to **Medium** (small, credibly bridgeable gap) or **Low** (the gap is the core of the job).
- *Calibration:* this rule would have rated two lost processes correctly before either began — one decided on data-product packaging experience, the other on creator and content environments.

**Rating: High / Medium / Low.** Not a precise number — the competition is usually invisible, and false precision would mislead.
- **High:** every selection-critical requirement met literally, direct pattern match, level fits.

**Level-mismatch cap.** A level mismatch in **either direction** — above or below where the role is targeted — caps candidate fit at **Medium**, even when every stated requirement is met literally. Over-levelling is a real selection risk, not a formality: One role was rated High on a full literal match while the level mismatch was noted in the same breath, and was lost on exactly that. Apply this cap **before** the requirements match can override it.
- **Medium:** selection-critical requirements met, or one met by credible analogy where the gap is small; other required qualifications largely met.
- **Low:** a selection-critical requirement missing or met only by analogy where it is the core of the job; or the role asks for a specific domain or product pattern not in the fact base.

**Known limitation — the unstated archetype.** Literal requirements can miss a preference the hiring team never wrote down, most commonly **executor versus strategist.** One process met every stated requirement and reached the final five, then was lost on "hands-on impact orientation and KPI-driven execution," which appeared nowhere in the JD. Literal-requirement scoring would have rated it High and been wrong. The selection-critical rule does not catch this. Test for it in the first conversation instead: *"What would the ideal person in this seat do differently from the last one?"* and *"What does a great first 90 days look like to you?"* — answers weighted toward hands-on delivery signal an executor archetype.

*Resolved later:* the archetype turned out to be more specific than "executor" — the hire was an engineering leader.

*Second case — the same signal from the other side.* Another role met every stated requirement and was lost because recent work sat at director level for a role scoped as one product area, one team, no reports. The first wanted hands-on *technical* leadership; the second wanted hands-on *delivery* at one-team scope. **Recent work is read as leadership, and hands-on roles see that first.**

**Functional center of gravity.** Job titles and role descriptions can obscure which discipline the organization actually wants to dominate the seat. A role called Product, Product Development, Transformation, Digital, Platform or Operating Model may in practice be optimized for someone whose recent identity is primarily Product, Engineering, Commercial, Operations, Data, Design or another function.

Candidate fit should therefore ask not only whether the candidate meets the stated requirements, but **which professional archetype the organization is actually selecting for.** This matters especially when the role spans several functions or uses ambiguous language such as "hands-on", "execution-oriented", "technical", "commercial" or "product development".
- **Primary probe:** *"When you picture the strongest candidate for this role, what function have they most likely spent the last five years leading?"*
- **Supporting probe:** *"If you had two excellent candidates, one stronger in product strategy and customer discovery and one stronger in engineering execution and delivery, which would be more useful to you right now?"*
- **Diagnostic:** *"What percentage of this role is deciding what and why, versus driving how and when?"*

If the functional center of gravity differs materially from the candidate's **recent career identity**, candidate fit is reduced even when the stated requirements appear to match. Compare against recent identity, not historical capability: the market reads the last five years.

*Reading heuristic — "product development."* In Finnish usage, **tuotekehitys / product development very often means R&D and engineering, not product management.** A title such as "Head of Product Development" can be an engineering leadership role in product language. When you see it, the first question is which of the two they mean: *"Is this role accountable for what gets built and why, or for how the engineering organization builds it?"*

**Hands-on is not one thing.** A manager getting hands-on again is part of the current definition of a strong product builder, so hands-on does not inherently mean IC-level scope. Determine what the organization actually means:
- **Hands-on Product** — personally engaging with customers, product evidence, prototypes, metrics, prioritization and product decisions. Compatible with senior scope; the spikes apply.
- **Hands-on Delivery** — personally driving a team, backlog, milestones and execution cadence. If that means one team with no reports, the level-mismatch cap applies.
- **Hands-on Technical** — personally engaging with architecture, implementation, engineering trade-offs and technical delivery. A functional-center-of-gravity mismatch (Engineering), not a level question.

These are different archetypes and are **not interchangeable.**
- **Probe:** *"When you say hands-on, what would I personally be doing in an ordinary week?"*
- **Follow with:** *"Do you mean close to customers and product decisions, close to delivery execution, or close to the technical implementation itself?"*

Calibration: one role looked closer to hands-on Product; another explicitly wanted hands-on Delivery at one-team scope; a third was, in retrospect, weighted toward hands-on Technical and engineering execution far more heavily than understood at the time. The classification changes candidate fit materially. It changes organizational fit **only** when it reveals that the seat itself is different from the one scored — that is new evidence about the role, not a reaction to the rejection.

**Reported alongside, not part of the rating — channel strength.** Four levels, and only the top two count:

| Level | Definition | Counts for the action? |
|---|---|---|
| **None** | No one inside. | No |
| **Contact** | Knows you; may refer you or pass on your CV. | **No** |
| **Advocate** | Explicitly tells the hiring team *why* your non-obvious background fits. | Yes |
| **Sponsor** | Has enough standing to materially influence who gets considered. | Yes |

*Contact is not advocacy.* One role had two internal contacts and was lost at application. Friendly people passing on a CV do not make the analogy argument. And even genuine advocacy does not overcome a missing selection-critical requirement — another had three advocates, the recruiter confirmed they were actively advocating, and it was still lost on channel and business-development experience. Advocacy gets the analogy **heard**; it does not make it **accepted**.

Channel strength never changes the candidate fit rating. It affects only the **action** below.

**The combined reading uses the organizational verdict, not a binary.** A Viable role, by definition, does not clearly beat the real alternative — so it cannot share an action with a Strong role.

| Organizational verdict | **Candidate High** | **Candidate Medium** | **Candidate Low** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strong** | **Pursue hard.** Best use of effort. | **Pursue, warm route preferred.** | **Advocate or sponsor first**, then apply. The CV alone will not carry the analogy; a better cover letter will not fix it. |
| **Viable** | **Pursue selectively.** Worth real effort, not your best effort. | **Low-cost pursuit only.** Apply if cheap; do not build a campaign. | **Only with an advocate or sponsor AND asymmetry 2.** Otherwise pass. |
| **Pass** | ⚠ **Danger zone.** Easy to get, bad to have. This is where a thin board tempts you. | Pass. | Pass. |

*Watchlist and Gig follow their own rules and sit outside the matrix.*

*"Compelling upside" is defined as asymmetry 2 deliberately.* A vaguer standard would be exactly the phrase a thin board stretches to justify a weak application.

**Rules:**
- Candidate fit **never** feeds the organizational total and **never** modifies a factor score.
- Channel strength **never** changes the candidate fit rating. Only Advocate and Sponsor count toward the action.
- A high candidate fit is **never** a reason to apply to a Pass role. The framework exists to prevent taking the gettable wrong job.
- Strong with low candidate fit is not a pass — it is an instruction about *channel*. Find the advocate first, apply second.
- Before investing in any role where candidate fit is Medium or Low, name the selection-critical requirement likely to decide it. It is usually one already on the gap list.
- Placing a role in a cell does not replace the diagnostic questions. The matrix says whether to pursue; the diagnostics say what to test.

**Outcome-update rule.** Selection outcomes update **candidate-fit calibration only.** A rejection does not retrospectively lower organizational fit or asymmetry — unless the process itself revealed **new organizational evidence** (an owner overriding a hiring manager, a role dissolved by reorganization, a mandate described differently in interview than in the JD). *"They rejected me, therefore we overrated the role"* is exactly the error this rule exists to prevent: rejections are evidence about how you are read, not about what the environment is like.

## 4. SCORED FACTORS (sum to 100%)

### Load-bearing (62%)

*Rule: any load-bearing factor ≤4 fires a warning. **Two or more load-bearing factors ≤3 = presumptive Pass (do not pursue), regardless of total.** (Capital-P "Pass" = the verdict meaning decline, not "clears the bar.") A high total does not fix a load-bearing failure.*

**Epistemic fit — 19%**
Does the organization believe outcomes matter more than output? Does it manage uncertainty with evidence, treat discovery as real work, and update when reality contradicts the plan? Distinguish genuine product epistemics from vocabulary.
> *Sensemaking:* high-epistemic organizations reliably distinguish **signal vs noise, uncertainty vs failure, activity vs progress, reversible vs irreversible.** An organization that cannot make these distinctions cannot perceive its own situation accurately, whatever vocabulary it uses.
>
> *Challenge without social threat:* can people challenge assumptions, evidence, reasoning, and strategy directly without turning disagreement into interpersonal conflict? High epistemic fit requires not only permission to disagree, but enough trust that disagreement improves the decision rather than damaging the relationship.
>
> *Curator test.* When building becomes cheap, product shifts from *"is this worth building?"* to *"is this worth shipping?"* — build five, ship one. **Does working software function as evidence, or as commitment?** A feature having been built is not evidence it deserves to ship.
> **Probe:** *"Tell me about something you recently prototyped or built and deliberately chose not to ship. What did you learn?"* If everything built must ship because effort has been spent, the organization is still running assembly-line economics. Much harder to game than asking whether they "do discovery."

**Translation tax — 17%** *(historically the most personally expensive factor)*
Energy spent explaining, aligning, reframing, and mediating versus building and deciding. High score = low tax. The trap: roles where "influence across the organization," "matrix intersection," "escalation point," and "manage expectations" *are* the job. Bounded translation (consulting with exit points, a defined remit) differs from permanent structural translation.

**Political safety / belonging — 14%**
Can you operate as yourself — show reality, disagree, bring your full range — without it being weaponized or making you the perpetual exception?
> **Sub-factor: International absorption capacity.** Does the day-to-day operating reality have a native place for an international operator, or would you be admired but not absorbed? Score the operating reality; ownership labels and stated ambition are weak proxies for it.
> - Working language genuinely English in daily work, not "English when needed."
> - Foreign senior people are structurally normal, not symbolic.
> - Markets and customers genuinely international or multi-country.
> - Decision-making does not rely on implicit local cultural codes you are locked out of.
> - Directness read as clarity rather than aggression.
> - Business and product debate happens openly, without required social choreography.
> - Values international perspective without reducing you to "the international one."
>
> *Catches a slow-burn pattern: strong personal warmth combined with low absorption capacity. A gradual depletion risk rather than a fast failure — easy to miss until months in.*

**Sustainability — 12%**
Sustainability is not low intensity. It is the ability of effort to generate durable value: customer outcomes, organizational capability, credible learning, stronger relationships, improved future decisions. A demanding environment can be sustainable when effort compounds. An apparently comfortable environment can be corrosive when work is repeatedly neutralized, forgotten, or converted only into reassurance and ceremony. Includes recoverability, travel, pace, emotional toll, and whether the real performance bar assumes sustained overextension.
> **AI throughput illusion.** Building speed can rise much faster than customer adoption, onboarding, organizational judgment, strategy, trust and behaviour change — all of which still move at human speed. An organization that expects human-speed work to accelerate in proportion to software production converts AI productivity into **overload rather than better products**: more roadmap commitments per person, not more exploration or higher quality.
> **Probe:** *"As AI has made some work faster, what have you deliberately chosen **not** to accelerate?"* Healthy answer: more exploration, higher quality, more discarded work, shorter feedback cycles. Warning answer: more commitments per person. *Connects to urgency displacement in the fear diagnostic — a frightened organization mistakes full utilization for safety, and AI makes full utilization cheaper to demand.*

### High weight (20%)

**Mandate — 11%**
**The score asks one question only: does accountability match usable authority?** Everything below is a *diagnostic for why the answer might be no* — do not apply five separate penalties for one underlying structural problem.
What can you actually decide, alone, without it returning up the chain? Real decision rights matching accountability.
> *A mandate can be advisory, coaching, or operating — but accountability must match the mandate type.* A coach does not need a Head of Product's decision rights; they fail on mandate only if held accountable for outcomes they can merely influence. Penalize mismatch between influence model and expected outcome, not a coaching role for lacking owned authority.
> *Gardener or foreman?* Organizations often recruit with gardener language — empowerment, autonomy, discovery — then demand foreman behaviour under pressure: centralized decisions, output control, visible short-term compliance. **A gardener is not passive.** Gardeners choose what to cultivate, prune what harms the system, allocate scarce resources, establish boundaries, and intervene when conditions deteriorate. The diligence question is whether the organization permits leaders to improve the conditions of performance, or only expects them to drive people harder inside unchanged conditions.
> *Structure is not the same as respect for structure.* A role, a decision right, a forum, or a governance process can exist formally and still mean nothing if the organization does not honour what it is for. The framework's other tests catch a promise with no structure behind it; this catches the inverse — a structure with no belief behind it. Test not only whether the structure exists, but whether people behave as though it means something.
> **Probe:** *"When did this role / process / forum last actually change an outcome?"* A structure nobody can point to changing anything is ceremonial, whatever the org chart says.
>
> *Where the profit formula lives (the mechanism, and the probe for newly created roles).* Product work manages four risks: user value, usability, feasibility, and business viability. The first three can usually be addressed within the product-development system. **Business viability additionally depends on the wider organization's ability to capture the value through its profit formula** — which sits outside that system.
>
> **Core diagnostic: What does this business actually make money from, and what kind of product behaviour does that profit formula make rational?**
>
> Ask the first half plainly and listen for what they cannot say without hesitating. Then work out the second half yourself — it is a prediction about your future working life, not something they will tell you. Two sub-tests follow from it:
> - **Line-independence vs portfolio synergy:** *"Does the profit formula depend on the business lines being independently healthy, or on synergies across the portfolio?"* If the former, a cross-cutting mandate is structurally weak regardless of title. P&L owners are accountable for their own line and will rationally decline to subordinate it to a portfolio thesis nobody has demonstrated. The predictable end state is reclassification: a central product function that cannot show it changes the profit formula gets treated as overhead, and the question shifts from business value to IT cost. (Both failed-fit cases in my calibration set ended this way.)
> - **Counterfactual availability:** see Learning Loops. Where switching is rare and cycles are long, product value cannot be demonstrated, and delivery becomes the only legible measure.
>
> Nothing here requires bad faith. This is also the substitute for the "when did it last change an outcome" probe when the role is newly created and has no track record to point at.
>
> *Where the P&L sits relative to the outcome.* **Ask: where does the P&L sit relative to the outcome I would be accountable for, and who owns the function whose behaviour has to change for that outcome to happen?** If those are different people and neither reports to you, the mandate is decorative regardless of title. This is sharper than "what can you decide alone," because it locates the specific gap rather than testing authority in general.
>
> *The routed problem.* Some vacancies exist because a leadership team has a known structural conflict it will not resolve — realigning incentives across functions is expensive and politically costly, while routing the problem to a person is free. The role then becomes the place where an unowned contradiction is stored, and when it does not resolve, the failure reads as the person's rather than the structure's.
> **Probe:** *"What did the previous holder of this role find hardest?"* Listen for whether the answer describes **a problem or a person.** A problem the organization owns is a challenge; a problem the organization has been routing to whoever holds the seat is a vacancy shaped like a piñata. Follow-up: *"What has the leadership team tried to change about that, and what did it cost them?"* If the answer is nothing, the cost is still waiting to be paid by whoever takes the job.

**Learning loops — 9%**
Does feedback exist *and* does the organization demonstrably update on it? Both conditions. Metrics without behaviour change is still a watermelon. Consumer and marketplace loops are tight; B2G, enterprise governance, and pre-PMF loops are slow and mediated.
> *Counterfactual availability — the structural test.* **Can product value be demonstrated empirically here, or only asserted?** Where switching is rare and customer cycles are long, no meaningful experiment or counterfactual is available. With no empirical measure of value, the only legible measure remaining is **delivery against plan** — so product management collapses into delivery management regardless of what the role description says. This is not a culture failure; it is what a business with unmeasurable product outcomes rationally falls back on. It predicts the "where is what was promised three years ago" question, budget-spreadsheet planning, and demands for monthly shipping receipts.
> **Probe:** *"How do you know when a product decision here was right? Can you point to a case where the data changed the answer?"* An organization that can only answer with delivery milestones has told you what it can actually measure.
>
> **Experimentation latitude / tooling absorption capacity.** *Can a competent employee safely experiment with strategically important technology without fighting the organization?* The word doing the work is **safely** — this is not an argument against security, privacy or compliance constraints, which are real. The diagnostic is whether governance **distinguishes**: sensitive production data from synthetic or tightly bounded data, and irreversible high-risk deployment from cheap reversible experiment. Organizations that cannot make those distinctions prohibit broadly, because prohibiting is easier than distinguishing.
> **Probes:**
> - How long does it take someone to go from an idea to a safe working prototype?
> - Are there approved sandboxes and paved roads, or does every experiment require an exception?
> - Can teams prototype with synthetic or non-sensitive data?
> - Does security help teams find a compliant way to experiment, or only control access?
> - Can a team obtain a new capability in days, weeks, or quarters?
> - What proportion of an announced transformation can ordinary product and engineering people actually use?
>
> *Connection to soil before water:* a company can announce programmes, hire consultants, publish strategy slides and buy licences, and still have permissioning and IT processes that prevent ordinary people from learning through use. That is watering harder into soil that absorbs nothing — and it is a stronger anti-theatre test than "are you using AI?"
>
> **Prototype-to-decision latency.** Experimentation latitude is half the test; the other half is what happens next. **How quickly can a team move from uncertainty → working artifact → customer or internal evidence → decision?** Fast prototyping is worthless if governance then waits six weeks to decide. This is the most reliable way to tell a genuinely AI-native product organization from one that bought licences.
> **Probes:** *"How long from an important product question to something people can actually interact with?"* and *"How often can teams throw a prototype away without it being treated as wasted delivery?"

### Standard (18%)

**Work interest — 5%** — Genuinely interesting domain *and* activity. Separate the two: the activity can score high while the domain drains.

**Culture / good faith — 5%** — Honest, respectful, good-faith people. Score only what is not already in political safety.
> *Kind, not merely nice.* Kindness combines respect and generosity with the willingness to give difficult feedback, challenge weak reasoning, and hold high standards. Niceness that avoids necessary conflict is not evidence of a healthy culture; neither is harshness evidence of high performance. What you are screening for is **high standards + low interpersonal threat** — kind and demanding. This guards against two opposite false positives: *"everyone is lovely here"* can hide conflict avoidance and learned helplessness; *"we have a very high-performance culture"* can hide fear, ego, and cruelty.
>
> *Evaluate the incentive system, not just the individuals* — decent people inside a poorly designed system can collectively produce harm, and personal warmth tends to disarm vigilance about the structure behind it. Niceness is often the mechanism: it removes the correcting signal that would let a system of good people notice what it is doing.

**Growth & optionality — 8%** — Does it compound the trajectory: learning, scope, credibility, narrative, doors? Excludes compensation (that is a gate).
> *Spike amplification with adjacent stretch.* The strongest Home role repeatedly exploits existing spikes while expanding the **next adjacent capability** the trajectory needs. It should not primarily hire you to remediate a weakness.
> - *Desirable pattern:* data, customer and UX judgment, product direction and stakeholder alignment — with growing ownership of business outcomes, pricing, unit economics and executive strategy.
> - *Undesirable pattern:* taking a largely sales, operations or finance role to acquire a missing badge.
> *"Don't chase your gap" and closing a selection-critical evidence gap do not conflict.* Choosing a **role** to patch a weakness is the error. Building evidence for a gap the market selects on — through work you own outside any role, such as the jobs-agent — is not a role choice at all.
> *The target, stated plainly:* a role paid primarily for judgment — making sense of customer, data and technology possibilities, creating a compelling direction, using artifacts and experiments to make choices tangible, and aligning an organization around the few things worth shipping. AI should increase the number and quality of options, not turn you into a ticket generator or a pseudo-engineer.
>
> *Upward judgment architecture — two different questions.* **Can they see the quality of my work?** (survival) and **can they make the quality of my thinking better?** (compounding). A senior person can have ample autonomy and still be in danger because nobody with enough authority can recognise whether their judgment is good.
>
> *Upward learning gradient / manager respect test.* **If I work successfully for this person for three years, what will I know, understand, or be capable of that I am not today?** Strip out age, title, and pedigree — the test is judgment, not seniority. A founder several years younger who has scaled two businesses, understands unit economics better than you, decides clearly and is secure enough to let you be the stronger product expert offers a steep gradient. A long-tenured leader who has spent twenty years managing roadmaps and budgets may offer none.
> **Probes:**
> - What is this person genuinely better than me at? Product craft need not be the answer — business, technology, organizational judgment, commercial thinking, leadership, or domain depth all count.
> - Can they challenge me intelligently — not overrule, but expose something I hadn't seen?
> - Do I trust their judgment when we disagree enough to think *"there is probably something here worth understanding"*? (Respect, not chemistry. These are different, and rapport is the weaker signal.)
> - Are they secure around expertise they don't possess — can they hire someone better than themselves at a discipline without being threatened?
> - What have their strongest reports gone on to do?
> - Do they sponsor, contextualize and protect, or merely supervise?
>
> *Evaluability (the survival half).* Separately from learning: **can anyone above me actually assess this work?** A manager who cannot evaluate product judgment will evaluate proxies instead — activity, delivery, visibility, responsiveness. That is the mechanism by which product work becomes delivery reporting regardless of stated intent. Probe: *how do the people above this role judge whether product work is good?* And the fragility check: *if my sponsor left tomorrow, who else could evaluate and defend my work?*
>
> *Architecture, not one person.* The direct manager need not be your product mentor. What the reporting architecture must contain is judgment that stretches yours, and credible access to it. A less-experienced direct manager is workable when a strong evaluator sits above and is genuinely reachable — and becomes untenable the moment that person leaves. The failure mode when neither exists: managing upward turns into another translation job, and you spend your time teaching product upward rather than learning anything.
>
> **Rule:** for a **Home** role, an upward learning gradient ≤4 is a serious warning; if the direct manager offers little gradient *and* there is no credible senior alternative through skip-level access, sponsorship, or peers, **Growth & Optionality cannot score above 5.** For a **Gig**, loosen it — the client problem itself can be the teacher. Over several years, though, you are not only choosing a job; you are choosing who gets to shape your thinking.

> *Capability compounding / relative skill decay.* A good environment compounds not only internal value but **externally relevant capability**. Effort should compound — and so should learning, which means the organization should not stand between you and the tools required for that compounding.
> **Probe:** *"If an important technology changes my profession over the next three years, will this environment let me learn it through use, or only observe it through governance?"*
> The risk is relative, not absolute. A year behind a restrictive firewall does not make an experienced leader unemployable. Several years while the market's skill baseline moves produces **relative capability decay**. Consider two comparable senior product people over three years: one prototyping with agents, learning evaluation, using coding assistants, seeing failure modes first-hand, redesigning workflows around what actually works; the other attending steering committees, tooling presentations, approval processes and quarterly transformation updates. Both can write the same words on a CV. They have not accumulated the same career capital.
> *Connection to translation tax:* in a restrictive environment you do not spend the time learning the technology — you spend it negotiating permission to learn the technology.
>
> *Career-path permeability / pedigree lock-in.* Can a strong lateral hire actually enter the leadership succession system, or does an internal caste's early pedigree continue to dominate decades later? Companies that build leadership from a graduate or trainee pipeline create an identifiable cohort: broad early exposure across divisions and countries, an internal network accumulated young, and a high-potential label applied before much has been demonstrated. The advantage is often **genuine** — that breadth can make someone better prepared — but it compounds through a Matthew effect: early selection leads to better assignments, wider network, more sponsorship, stronger CV, and finally evidence that the original selection was correct. The bias at decision time is familiarity, not favouritism.
> **Probes (both hard to answer with spin):**
> - *"Looking at people who joined laterally in the last five years, who has since moved into major group leadership roles?"*
> - *"Where did the current leadership team enter the company?"* If six of ten executives entered through the same programme, school, HQ country or consultancy, that is information; you do not need them to interpret it for you.
> - Are high-potential labels revisited, or do they become permanent status? Do externally hired senior leaders receive comparable sponsorship and developmental assignments?
>
> *Interaction with international absorption (important).* **An international company is not the same as an international leadership market.** A company can run entirely in English with people from forty nationalities and still operate: HQ-country insiders → strategy, succession and group leadership; country-market people → excellent execution and local leadership. Ask whether someone hired in the country organisation has a credible route to a group executive role, not just whether the meetings are in English.
> *Interaction with sponsorship.* **Sponsorship gets you into consideration; career-path permeability determines whether the system can absorb you upward.** A sponsor may genuinely back you and still be fighting institutional gravity.
> *Honest calibration:* even in open systems familiarity carries weight, and you will never compete on fully equal terms with someone who has been inside five years. What you are testing is not the absence of bias — it does not exist — but whether the door is structurally closed. The difference between *"harder from outside"* and *"nobody comes from outside"* is what matters.

## 5. FEAR DIAGNOSTIC (causal overlay, not separately scored)

**Rule:** the Fear Diagnostic is **not independently scored**. It identifies the likely common cause beneath several factor scores. High-confidence evidence of chronic fear must be reflected in the affected factor scores (typically political safety, sustainability, translation tax, epistemic fit, mandate), and can trigger a presumptive Pass when the pattern is systemic rather than episodic. Scoring it separately would double-count it.

**Distinguish two kinds:**
- **Situational fear** — a temporary crisis with proportionate response and a credible route back to normal. Not disqualifying.
- **Structural fear** — persistent threat monitoring, inflated magnitude, defensive escalation, centralized control, punishment of uncomfortable information. Only this functions as a major disqualifier.

Chronic structural fear tends to degrade several otherwise positive factors simultaneously. In such environments, people who surface uncomfortable reality are likely to be marginalized, contained, or treated as the source of the discomfort, regardless of the role's advertised mandate.

**Detection probes (inflated magnitude battery):**
- Ask about a recent crisis and listen for **proportionality** in the telling.
- Ask how quickly a **cheap, reversible** decision can be made.
- Listen for whether anyone can say **"this is not especially important."**
- Ask **what stopped or returned to normal after the crisis ended.** *(Healthy systems mobilize intensely and then stand down. Fear systems retain emergency governance after the emergency has passed — this probe distinguishes intensity from dysfunction.)*

**Trust in expertise (strong form):** not "do leaders value experts?" but **"what happens when expert advice contradicts leadership's initial intuition?"** A system that routinely overrides its own hired expertise creates the bottleneck that makes everything feel urgent, which reinforces the distrust. The positive criterion: an organization that believes its experts and gives them room is where effort compounds.

**Urgency displacement (strategic slack):** urgent work is legible; strategic opportunity cost is counterfactual and invisible, so it gets postponed without anyone observing a failure today. Probes:
- *"What has this company recently chosen **not** to do, in order to do something more important?"* An organization that cannot answer has told you where its slack went.
- *"How does strategic work, learning or customer exposure get scheduled — inside working capacity, or around the edges?"* Treating it as extracurricular reveals the operating priority rather than the stated one.
- **Externally observable proxy:** does the company show up where its buyers are — industry events, customer forums, the places relationships form? If competitors are present and it is not because everyone is too busy, that is the reactive-capacity trap visible from outside. *Caveat: declining low-ROI events is good prioritization. The test is whether such choices are evaluated on expected value, or reflexively declined for lack of capacity.*

**Related frame — financial gravity (Ries, *Incorruptible*):** control-buyout ownership structures with hard margin targets tend to pull even principled leaders toward short-termism and control over empowerment. Ownership is a pressure signal, not a culture verdict; the label spans a wide range, and a minority stake differs materially from a control recapitalization.

## 6. SOIL VERSUS WATER (portable heuristic)

Organizations reach for **water** (interventions: AI, CRM, OKRs, tooling, product ops, new operating models) when the question is whether the **soil** (conditions, incentives, trust, authority) can sustain growth.

> **Are they improving the soil, or watering harder?**

- Does this tool fix the problem, or amplify existing dysfunction?
- Does new tooling fix this, or document the confusion?
- Does a new operating model matter if incentives stay the same?
- Does hiring a great PM matter if product authority does not exist?

*This is a fast diagnostic, not a theory, and not an anti-tool stance. Sometimes water genuinely is the missing input. The heuristic asks whether the proposed intervention addresses the actual constraint.*

## 7. POSTING-DELETIONS RULE

When postings can be compared across time, **deleted promises can be more diagnostic than retained language** — particularly when the deletions align with changes in reporting lines, responsibilities, governance, or leadership. Treat deletion as evidence to investigate, not proof by itself; deletions can also result from copy editing, a different recruiter, or a narrower brief.

For any single posting: **for each promising phrase, is there structural evidence the organization can deliver it** — a clear mandate, a stated reporting line, real decision rights, a sponsor with authority, an ownership model that is not purely cost-and-control? Where a promise floats free of structure, that is the warning. Aspirational language often reflects a sponsor's intent rather than the executive layer's commitment.

## 8. WARNING AND EVIDENCE CHECKS

**Evidence provenance.** Every factor score above 6 requires at least one piece of **behavioural or structural** evidence. Advertised language alone cannot justify a high score.
- **Advertised** — job posting, careers page, employer branding.
- **Reported** — recruiter statements, interview claims, internal contact accounts.
- **Demonstrated** — concrete examples, repeated behaviour, actual decision rights, organizational history, observed process.

*"Empowered product culture" in a posting is advertised. A leader describing a recent decision a team made against executive preference is demonstrated.*

**Core diagnostic question (tests Epistemic fit and Culture simultaneously):**

> *"Tell me about a time someone strongly disagreed with a senior leader here. What happened to the argument, and what happened to the relationship afterward?"*

The desired answer is **not** necessarily that the challenger won. It is that:
- the disagreement was explicit rather than absorbed or deflected,
- evidence was actually examined,
- somebody updated, or the decision was made clearly and openly,
- nobody was socially punished,
- and the working relationship remained intact afterward.

That combination is high-performance trust. An interviewer who cannot produce any such example — or who produces one where the challenger was subsequently sidelined — has answered the question.

*In-room test (no question required):* does anyone in the process actually challenge you? A kind-and-demanding organization will push back on something you say during interviews. A nice one nods through everything and tells you afterward how much they enjoyed the conversation. This doubles as a corrective to the rapport trap — being liked and never challenged is a warning, not a signal.

**Other checks:**
- **Confidence (H/M/L) per factor.** L-heavy scores mean "go verify," not "it's fine." Inside reads from someone who works there are the highest-value way to convert L to H.
- **Pitfall stress test** — any load-bearing factor ≥7: name the scenario that would reveal it is actually a 4, and the early canary.
- **What would prove this wrong** — any total >6: name the disqualifier and when you would know.
- **Real alternative** — not the next-best job. What would you actually do if you passed? Keeps marginal roles compared against an honest baseline rather than against hope.

---

## 9. USAGE NOTES (the evaluator is also a cognitive system)

- **Score when emotionally regulated.** State shapes the quality and range of cognition. A depleted mind does not necessarily produce false thoughts, but it changes which explanations feel obvious and which possibilities remain visible. Do not finalize a score — or a self-assessment — from a depleted state.
- **Rapport is weak evidence of structural fit.** Positive interpersonal chemistry indicates rapport, not organizational fit. Being liked and admired tends to disarm vigilance; treat warmth as pleasant, not as signal.

---

## 10. VERDICT LABELS

- **Strong** — passes gates, beats the real alternative, no load-bearing warnings.
- **Viable** — passes gates, does not clearly beat the real alternative; hold lightly.
- **Watchlist** — insufficient evidence or not live enough; monitor, do not pursue.
- **Pass** — fails gates, loses to the real alternative, or fires the double load-bearing warning.
- **Gig** — worth a time-boxed tour (credential, experience, income, optionality), not a home. Requires: secured runway, other live threads, honest expectation of exit, compensation that clears the floor.
- **Real alternative / self-generated platform** — the independent, advisory, or board path; the benchmark other roles compete against.

---

## 11. CLOSING QUESTIONS

**For a potential home:** Is this organization worthy of several years of my life and full emotional investment?

**For a deliberate gig:** Is this opportunity worthy of a bounded period of excellent work, under explicit terms, with a clear reason for entering and leaving?

**If neither answer is yes — Pass.**

## 12. CALIBRATION APPENDIX

*The framework above is meant to stay stable; the appendix is where dated, real cases live, and it is what keeps the scoring honest. My own appendix is private, because it names organizations and people. What it has taught so far, anonymized:*

- **Two failed-fit shapes.** A structure emptied of meaning over time, and a structure created without the belief needed to sustain it. For a newly created role, the mandate probe becomes: *"What makes you confident the business units will defer to a central product function?"*
- **A stated rejection reason can be a socially safe proxy.** Test it against later evidence rather than against the explanation given at the time. Not every rejection is a dodged bullet; some are genuine losses to closer fits.
- **Organizational fit does not predict selection.** In every lost process where the reason was known, the deciding factor was a literal stated requirement already on the gap list, or a level or functional-archetype mismatch — never the quality of the analogy. Asymmetry can be real as a statement about value creation and still predict nothing about hiring.
- **The squeeze.** Senior roles went to candidates with the literal domain; hands-on roles went to candidates whose recent work was hands-on in the form the role meant (technical in one case, delivery in another). Selection is won where senior level and a literal match line up. This is a working heuristic from a small sample, not a law.
- **Sponsorship is not permeability.** A hiring manager who wants you, argues for you and wins the budget can still lose the decision to owners who are moving the centre of gravity elsewhere.
- **Rapport predicted little.** Warmth and interest repeatedly failed to convert, or converted into roles without structural fit.

*To build your own:* for each case, record the verdict you gave before the outcome, the outcome, the stated reason, and anything learned later that changed the reading. Update candidate-fit calibration from outcomes; update organizational fit only when the process revealed new evidence about the organization.
